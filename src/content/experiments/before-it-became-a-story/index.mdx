The teacher couldn’t seem to find the right words. I was sitting a few rows back. All morning, he stood tall, shoulders squared, assigning seats and explaining the 2nd grade curriculum and expectations for the rest of the semester. His voice carried easily to the back of the classroom. Now, his shoulders were slightly, uncomfortably slumped, his hands gesturing something in front of him, like he was explaining something to himself.

He was asking the class a question, and I listened intently, trying to be a good student. He cleared his throat, “Ahem… Government benefit… disability… low income… if you could raise your hand… or you don’t have to, but if you don’t mind raising your hand…”

A pause. I raised my hand up high.

The teacher scanned the room quickly, and looked me in the eye, acknowledging with a nod and a smile. I couldn’t tell what he meant by that smile. And then he quickly gestured to have me lower my hand, as if he were afraid the other students would see my raised hand.

I wasn’t quite sure what was going on. I thought I followed the instructions correctly? Is this something I need to keep secret?

My mom always talked about the government benefit we received with a cheerful voice. “It means extra money!” And my sister and I LOVED it when our car drove past all the cars at busy parking lots, when other cars were fighting over spots, and we could roll up and park right by the front door.

Huh.

I lowered my hand.

</Essay>

<Afterword label="Notes">

This is a piece I wrote for Storytelling Workshop 2.0. I liked what I learned from the first workshop so much that I decided to take the 2.0 version. Writing doesn’t come naturally to me, though. Each class, I muster up all my strength to see if I can produce something worth sharing.

The prompt was “The Part I Usually Leave Out”: a story you’ve told many times, but the version you don’t tell. I dug around and turned over stones that had been left alone for years. There was so much I wanted to say. While jotting down notes and phrases, I made myself cry. The story I wanted to tell was much larger than the 300-word limit, and I found it impossible to stay in the moment without reflecting and adding hindsight. I almost didn’t finish. Then, in the shower, a lightbulb went on: what if I tell only this one scene, from when I was a second grader? To my surprise, the scene without explanation and reflection turned out more powerful than the version where I felt like I said it all.

What got me interested in this workshop in the first place was the usefulness of the storytelling skills. The applications seemed limitless. But I think when I arrived at the end of the workshop series I had discovered something else. When I listened to my classmates’ stories, each person became more 3D. I got to see sides of them that might never come up in everyday conversations, even with people I’ve known for years. We ended the class with camaraderie, like the last day at camp. I think that level of human connection might just be the heart of storytelling.

</Afterword>
